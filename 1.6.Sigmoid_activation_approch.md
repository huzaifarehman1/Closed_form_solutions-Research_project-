# Exact Sigmoid Stationarity Approach


---

# 1. Network Architecture

Consider a two-layer neural network with an arbitrary number of hidden neurons:

$$
X
\rightarrow
W_1
\rightarrow
\sigma
\rightarrow
W_2
\rightarrow
\hat Y
$$

where

$$
X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d},
$$

$$
W_1\in\mathbb R^{d\times h},
$$

$$
W_2\in\mathbb R^{h\times 1},
$$

and

$$
Y\in\mathbb R^{n\times1}.
$$

Here:

* \(n\) = number of training samples
* \(d\) = number of input features
* \(h\) = number of hidden neurons

The hidden-layer preactivation is

$$
Z=XW_1.
$$

The hidden representation is

$$
A=\sigma(Z)=\sigma(XW_1).
$$

The prediction is

$$
\boxed{
\hat Y=AW_2.
}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\hat Y=\sigma(XW_1)W_2.
}
$$

---

# 2. Loss Function

We use the squared-error objective:

$$
\boxed{
L(W_1,W_2)
=
\frac12
\|\sigma(XW_1)W_2-Y\|_2^2.
}
$$

Define the residual

$$
E=\hat Y-Y.
$$

Therefore,

$$
E=AW_2-Y.
$$

The optimization problem is

$$
\min_{W_1,W_2}
\frac12\|AW_2-Y\|_2^2.
$$

---

# 3. Key Observation: The Final Layer Is Linear

For any fixed \(W_1\), the hidden representation

$$
A=\sigma(XW_1)
$$

is fixed.

The network then becomes

$$
\hat Y=AW_2.
$$

Therefore, for fixed \(W_1\), finding \(W_2\) is simply a linear least-squares problem:

$$
\min_{W_2}
\frac12\|AW_2-Y\|_2^2.
$$

Its normal equation is

$$
A^T(AW_2-Y)=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
A^TAW_2=A^TY.
$$

If \(A^TA\) is invertible,

$$
\boxed{
W_2=(A^TA)^{-1}A^TY.
}
$$

More generally, using the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse,

$$
\boxed{
W_2=A^+Y.
}
$$

This is an exact global minimizer of the loss with respect to \(W_2\), for the given \(W_1\).

---

# 4. Analytical Elimination of \(W_2\)

This is the first major simplification.

Originally we need to solve

$$
\min_{W_1,W_2}L(W_1,W_2).
$$

But because \(W_2\) can be solved exactly for every \(W_1\),

$$
W_2(W_1)
=
[\sigma(XW_1)]^+Y.
$$

Therefore, the problem can be reduced to finding suitable \(W_1\).

Once \(W_1\) is known,

$$
\boxed{
W_2=
[\sigma(XW_1)]^+Y.
}
$$

Thus we no longer need to independently search over both weight matrices.

---

# 5. Exact Sigmoid Derivative

The sigmoid activation is

$$
\boxed{
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
}
$$

Its derivative is

$$
\sigma'(z)
=
\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)).
$$

Since

$$
A=\sigma(XW_1),
$$

we can write

$$
\boxed{
\sigma'(XW_1)=A(1-A)
}
$$

where the multiplication is element-wise.

No approximation is being made.

---

# 6. Exact Gradient With Respect to \(W_2\)

The loss is

$$
L=\frac12\|AW_2-Y\|^2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_{W_2}L
=
A^T(AW_2-Y).
}
$$

The stationarity condition is

$$
\boxed{
A^T(AW_2-Y)=0.
}
$$

This produces exactly the least-squares solution

$$
\boxed{
W_2=A^+Y.
}
$$

Thus the \(W_2\) stationarity condition is completely solved.

---

# 7. Exact Gradient With Respect to \(W_1\)

Let

$$
E=AW_2-Y.
$$

Using the chain rule,

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_{W_1}L
=
X^T
\left[
(EW_2^T)
\odot
\sigma'(XW_1)
\right].
}
$$

For sigmoid,

$$
\sigma'(XW_1)=A(1-A).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_{W_1}L
=
X^T
\left[
(EW_2^T)
\odot
A(1-A)
\right].
}
$$

Consequently, every stationary solution satisfies

$$
\boxed{
X^T
\left[
(EW_2^T)
\odot
A(1-A)
\right]
=
0.
}
$$

where

$$
A=\sigma(XW_1)
$$

and

$$
E=AW_2-Y.
$$

---

# 8. Eliminate \(W_2\) From the Stationarity Equation

Since

$$
W_2=A^+Y,
$$

we can substitute this into the \(W_1\) stationarity condition.

First,

$$
E
=
AA^+Y-Y.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
F(W_1)
=
X^T
\left[
\left(
(AA^+Y-Y)(A^+Y)^T
\right)
\odot
A(1-A)
\right].
}
$$

where

$$
A=\sigma(XW_1).
$$

The remaining problem is therefore

$$
\boxed{
F(W_1)=0.
}
$$

This is the central equation of the exact sigmoid approach.

---

# 9. Complete Reduction of the Neural Network Problem

The original optimization problem is

$$
\min_{W_1,W_2}
\frac12
\|
\sigma(XW_1)W_2-Y
\|^2.
$$

We can analytically eliminate \(W_2\):

$$
W_2=
[\sigma(XW_1)]^+Y.
$$

The remaining problem becomes:

$$
\boxed{
F(W_1)=0.
}
$$

Therefore the neural-network training problem has been transformed into a nonlinear system of equations.

The procedure is:

$$
\boxed{
W_1
\rightarrow
A=\sigma(XW_1)
\rightarrow
W_2=A^+Y
\rightarrow
F(W_1)
}
$$

and we search for

$$
F(W_1)=0.
$$

---

# 10. Multiple Stationary Solutions

The equation

$$
F(W_1)=0
$$

may have multiple solutions.

Suppose we find

$$
W_1^{(1)},W_1^{(2)},\ldots,W_1^{(k)}.
$$

For every solution,

$$
A_i=\sigma(XW_1^{(i)}).
$$

Then calculate

$$
\boxed{
W_2^{(i)}=A_i^+Y.
}
$$

Each pair

$$
(W_1^{(i)},W_2^{(i)})
$$

is a stationary candidate.

We then calculate its loss:

$$
\boxed{
L_i
=
\frac12
\|A_iW_2^{(i)}-Y\|^2.
}
$$

Finally select the candidate with the smallest loss:

$$
\boxed{
i^*
=
\arg\min_i L_i.
}
$$

and return

$$
\boxed{
W_1^*=W_1^{(i^*)},
\qquad
W_2^*=W_2^{(i^*)}.
}
$$

---

# 11. Candidate-Based Training

This changes the philosophy of neural-network training.

Traditional gradient-based training attempts to move toward a minimum:

$$
W\leftarrow W-\eta\nabla L.
$$

The proposed method instead attempts to find solutions of

$$
\boxed{
\nabla L=0.
}
$$

It can therefore be viewed as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Stationary-point generation}
\rightarrow
\text{candidate evaluation}
\rightarrow
\text{best-loss selection}.
}
$$

The objective is not to follow the loss downhill.

Instead, we attempt to generate multiple stationary solutions and then compare their exact losses.

---

# 12. Method 1 — Multi-Start Root Solving

The first proposed solution method is direct nonlinear root solving.

We have

$$
F(W_1)=0.
$$

Choose multiple initial matrices

$$
W_1^{(0,1)},
W_1^{(0,2)},
\ldots,
W_1^{(0,K)}.
$$

For each initial point, use a nonlinear equation solver to find a root of

$$
F(W_1)=0.
$$

This gives candidate solutions

$$
\mathcal S_1
=
\{
W_1^{(1)},
W_1^{(2)},
\ldots
\}.
$$

After obtaining the roots:

1. Calculate \(A_i=\sigma(XW_1^{(i)})\).
2. Calculate \(W_2^{(i)}=A_i^+Y\).
3. Calculate \(L_i\).
4. Sort candidates by loss.
5. Select the lowest-loss candidate.

The method is therefore

$$
\boxed{
\text{Many initial points}
\rightarrow
F(W_1)=0
\rightarrow
\text{stationary solutions}
\rightarrow
\text{loss comparison}.
}
$$

---

# 13. Important Distinction From Gradient Descent

Root solving and gradient descent are not the same operation.

Gradient descent solves an optimization problem iteratively:

$$
W_{k+1}
=
W_k-\eta\nabla L(W_k).
$$

The proposed root-solving method instead solves

$$
F(W_1)=\nabla_{W_1}L=0.
$$

A root solver attempts to make the equation itself equal to zero.

Therefore, the proposed method does not require a learning rate or a loss-minimization trajectory.

However, numerical root solvers are still iterative numerical algorithms. Therefore, the method should be described as **training without gradient-based optimization**, rather than claiming that it contains no numerical iteration whatsoever.

---

# 14. Method 2 — Homotopy Continuation

A second approach is to make the nonlinear stationarity equation gradually harder.

Instead of immediately solving

$$
F(W_1)=0,
$$

construct

$$
\boxed{
H(W_1,t)=0
}
$$

where

$$
t\in[0,1].
$$

At

$$
t=0,
$$

we define an easy problem whose solution is known.

At

$$
t=1,
$$

the system becomes the actual sigmoid stationarity equation:

$$
H(W_1,1)=F(W_1).
$$

A simple homotopy is

$$
\boxed{
H(W_1,t)
=
(1-t)(W_1-W_0)
+
tF(W_1).
}
$$

At \(t=0\),

$$
H(W_1,0)=W_1-W_0,
$$

whose solution is

$$
W_1=W_0.
$$

At \(t=1\),

$$
H(W_1,1)=F(W_1).
$$

Thus a solution can be tracked from the easy system toward the actual neural-network stationarity system.

---

# 15. Homotopy Path

The conceptual process is

$$
W_1(0)
\rightarrow
W_1(t_1)
\rightarrow
W_1(t_2)
\rightarrow
\cdots
\rightarrow
W_1(1).
$$

The final point satisfies

$$
F(W_1(1))=0.
$$

Different initial \(W_0\)'s can be used to explore different stationary solutions.

Thus the method becomes

$$
\boxed{
\text{Easy system}
\rightarrow
\text{continuation}
\rightarrow
\text{sigmoid stationarity root}.
}
$$

---

# 16. Method 1 vs Method 2

## Method 1: Multi-Start Root Solving

$$
\boxed{
W_1^{(0)}
\rightarrow
F(W_1)=0
}
$$

### Advantages

* Simple.
* Direct.
* Easy to implement.
* Potentially fast.
* Naturally parallelizable.
* Directly targets the exact stationarity equations.

### Limitations

* Some roots may not be discovered.
* Results can depend strongly on starting points.
* Nonlinear root solvers may fail.
* There is no general guarantee that every stationary point will be found.

---

## Method 2: Homotopy Continuation

$$
\boxed{
H(W_1,0)=0
\rightarrow
H(W_1,1)=F(W_1)=0
}
$$

### Advantages

* Can make difficult nonlinear systems easier to solve.
* Can track continuous solution branches.
* Potentially better at discovering difficult stationary solutions.
* Provides a more structured numerical approach.

### Limitations

* More computationally expensive.
* Requires continuation steps.
* Solution branches can be difficult or singular.
* A naive homotopy does not guarantee discovery of every root.

---

# 17. Hybrid Strategy

The strongest practical strategy may be to use both approaches.

Generate candidates using:

$$
\mathcal S_1
=
\text{MultiStartRootSolve}(F)
$$

and

$$
\mathcal S_2
=
\text{HomotopySolve}(F).
$$

Combine them:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal S
=
\mathcal S_1\cup\mathcal S_2.
}
$$

Remove duplicate solutions.

For each unique candidate:

$$
A_i=\sigma(XW_1^{(i)})
$$

$$
W_2^{(i)}=A_i^+Y
$$

$$
L_i=
\frac12
\|A_iW_2^{(i)}-Y\|^2.
$$

Then select

$$
\boxed{
(W_1^*,W_2^*)
=
\arg\min_{(W_1^{(i)},W_2^{(i)})\in\mathcal S}
L_i.
}
$$

---

# 18. What Has Been Solved

The exact sigmoid approach has solved several important components.

### Final-layer optimization

$$
\boxed{
W_2=A^+Y
}
$$

is solved exactly for every \(W_1\).

### Exact activation

The real sigmoid is retained:

$$
\boxed{
\sigma(z)=\frac1{1+e^{-z}}.
}
$$

No Taylor approximation is required.

### Exact gradient

The exact \(W_1\) gradient is known:

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_{W_1}L
=
X^T
[
((AW_2-Y)W_2^T)
\odot
A(1-A)
].
}
$$

### Exact stationarity equation

$$
\boxed{
F(W_1)=0.
}
$$

### Multiple-candidate strategy

Multiple stationary solutions can be generated and evaluated.

### Final solution selection

The candidate with the smallest observed loss can be selected.

---

# 19. What Has NOT Been Proven

It is important not to overclaim the method.

We have **not yet proven** that:

$$
\boxed{
\text{all stationary points can be found for arbitrary sigmoid networks.}
}
$$

We have also not proven that the lowest-loss stationary point found numerically is always the global minimum.

The current method is therefore better described as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Exact stationarity-based candidate generation}
}
$$

rather than a guaranteed global closed-form solution.

# 23. Important Scope

The current derivation concerns a two-layer network

$$
X\rightarrow W_1\rightarrow\sigma\rightarrow W_2\rightarrow Y
$$

with a single linear output layer and arbitrary hidden width.

The hidden layer can contain any number \(h\) of neurons:

$$
W_1\in\mathbb R^{d\times h}.
$$

The method is therefore not restricted to the one-hidden-neuron case.

Extension to deeper networks is a separate research question.
