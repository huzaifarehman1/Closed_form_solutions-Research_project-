# Analytical Optimization of Neural Networks with Absolute-Value Activation

## 1. Problem Setup

We consider a neural network of the form

$$
X
\longrightarrow
\text{Linear}
\longrightarrow
|\cdot|
\longrightarrow
\text{Linear}
$$

with an absolute-value activation function.

Let

$$
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},
\qquad
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},
$$

where:

* \(n\) = number of training samples,
* \(d\) = input dimension,
* \(m\) = output dimension.

For a hidden layer with \(h\) neurons, the network is

$$
H = |XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|,
$$

$$
\hat Y = HW_2+\mathbf{1}b_2^T,
$$

where

$$
W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times h},
\qquad
b_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h},
$$

$$
W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times m},
\qquad
b_2\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.
$$

The absolute value is applied element-wise.

Our objective is the minimum mean squared error:

$$
\min_{W_1,b_1,W_2,b_2}
\frac{1}{n}
\left\|
|XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|W_2
+
\mathbf{1}b_2^T
-
Y
\right\|_F^2.
$$

Since the factor \(1/n\) does not affect the optimizer, we can equivalently minimize the squared Frobenius norm:

$$
\boxed{
\min
\left\|
|XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|W_2
+
\mathbf{1}b_2^T
-
Y
\right\|_F^2
}
$$

---

# 2. Why the Absolute-Value Activation Creates the Difficulty

Without an activation, the network would reduce to linear regression.

For example,

$$
\hat Y=XW+\mathbf{1}b^T
$$

has the standard least-squares solution obtained using the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

The difficulty appears when we introduce

$$
H=|XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|.
$$

For a scalar \(z\),

$$
|z|=
\begin{cases}
z,&z\ge0,\\
-z,&z<0.
\end{cases}
$$

Therefore, the function is piecewise linear.

For a hidden neuron,

$$
h=|Xw+b\mathbf{1}|,
$$

each training sample can lie on either side of the zero boundary.

Consequently, the parameter space is divided into different activation/sign regions.

For a particular sign pattern

$$
s\in\{-1,+1\}^n,
$$

we have

$$
\operatorname{sign}(Xw+b\mathbf{1})=s.
$$

Within that region,

$$
|Xw+b\mathbf{1}|
=
\operatorname{diag}(s)(Xw+b\mathbf{1}).
$$

Thus, once the sign pattern is fixed, the absolute value becomes a linear transformation.

This leads to the central observation:

$$
\boxed{
\text{The nonconvexity is caused by the activation-induced partition of parameter space.}
}
$$

However, the absolute value is also highly structured because it is piecewise linear. This structure can be exploited to avoid gradient descent.

---

# 3. Scalar One-Neuron Case

Consider first the scalar-input, scalar-output network

$$
\hat y=w_2|w_1x+b_1|+b_2.
$$

We want to minimize

$$
L=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\left(
w_2|w_1x_i+b_1|+b_2-y_i
\right)^2.
$$

At first glance, it may appear tempting to simply set

$$
w_1=1.
$$

However, this must be justified rather than assumed.

---

# 4. Scaling Symmetry and the \(w_1=1\) Reparameterization
for  (w_1 > 0) or (w_1 < 0)



$$
|w_1x+b_1|
=
|w_1|
\left|
x+\frac{b_1}{w_1}
\right|.
$$

Define

$$
a=w_2|w_1|,
$$

and

$$
t=-\frac{b_1}{w_1}.
$$

Then

$$
w_2|w_1x+b_1|+b_2
=
a|x-t|+b_2.
$$

Therefore the network can be represented as

$$
\boxed{
\hat y=a|x-t|+b
}
$$

for suitable \(a,t,b\).

Conversely, any parameters \(a,t,b\) can be represented by choosing

$$
w_1=c,
$$

$$
b_1=-ct,
$$

$$
w_2=\frac{a}{|c|},
$$

$$
b_2=b,
$$

for any

$$
c\neq0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
w_1=1
}
$$

is not imposing a restriction on the represented functions.

It is simply fixing a scaling degree of freedom.

In particular,

$$
(w_1,b_1,w_2,b_2)
=
\left(
c,-ct,\frac{a}{|c|},b
\right)
$$

produces exactly the same function for every \(c\neq0\).

Thus \(w_1\) is not uniquely identifiable.

The case \(w_1=0\) produces a constant hidden activation and therefore a constant output, which is already representable within the reparameterized family.

Hence fixing \(w_1=1\) does not remove any achievable function or change the minimum MSE. It only removes redundant parameterizations.

---

# 5. Scalar Case: Reduction to a One-Dimensional Optimization

Using

$$
\hat y_i=a|x_i-t|+b,
$$

define

$$
z_i(t)=|x_i-t|.
$$

For a fixed \(t\), the model becomes ordinary linear regression:

$$
\hat y_i=az_i+b.
$$

Therefore \(a\) and \(b\) have closed-form least-squares solutions.

Let

$$
\bar z=\frac1n\sum_i z_i,
\qquad
\bar y=\frac1n\sum_i y_i.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
a(t)=
\frac{
\sum_i(z_i-\bar z)(y_i-\bar y)
}{
\sum_i(z_i-\bar z)^2
}
}
$$

provided the denominator is nonzero.

The intercept is

$$
\boxed{
b(t)=\bar y-a(t)\bar z.
}
$$

Thus the original four-parameter optimization reduces to

$$
\boxed{
t^*
=
\arg\min_t
\sum_i
\left(
a(t)|x_i-t|+b(t)-y_i
\right)^2.
}
$$

Therefore the scalar problem can be reduced to a one-dimensional optimization problem with an exact closed-form solution for the remaining linear parameters.

---

# 6. Matrix Generalization

Now consider the full problem

$$
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},
\qquad
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}.
$$

For a hidden layer of width \(h\),

$$
H=|XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|,
$$

and

$$
\hat Y=HW_2+\mathbf{1}b_2^T.
$$

The training objective is

$$
L=
\left\|
HW_2+\mathbf{1}b_2^T-Y
\right\|_F^2.
$$

At this stage, \(H\) depends on the unknown first-layer parameters.

---

# 7. Exact Solution of the Output Layer

For fixed \(W_1,b_1\), the hidden representation

$$
H=|XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|
$$

is completely known.

We can then solve the output layer exactly.

Augment the hidden matrix with a bias column:

$$
\tilde H=
\begin{bmatrix}
H & \mathbf{1}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Similarly define

$$
\tilde W_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
W_2\\
b_2^T
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
\hat Y=\tilde H\tilde W_2.
$$

The optimization becomes

$$
\min_{\tilde W_2}
\|\tilde H\tilde W_2-Y\|_F^2.
$$

The minimum-norm least-squares solution is

$$
\boxed{
\tilde W_2^*=\tilde H^+Y
}
$$

where \(\tilde H^+\) is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\begin{bmatrix}
W_2^*\\
b_2^{*T}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\tilde H^+Y.
}
$$

This means the entire output layer can be eliminated analytically.

---

# 8. Minimum Loss After Eliminating the Output Layer

The orthogonal projection onto the column space of \(\tilde H\) is

$$
P_{\tilde H}
=
\tilde H\tilde H^+.
$$

Therefore the optimal prediction is

$$
\hat Y^*
=
P_{\tilde H}Y.
$$

The minimum achievable training loss for a given hidden representation is

$$
\boxed{
L^*(W_1,b_1)
=
\left\|
(I-P_{\tilde H})Y
\right\|_F^2
}
$$

where

$$
H=|XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|.
$$

Hence the original optimization reduces to

$$
\boxed{
\min_{W_1,b_1}
\left\|
(I-P_{\tilde H})Y
\right\|_F^2.
}
$$

This is an exact reduction.

---

# 9. What Has Been Solved and What Remains

We have completely solved the second linear layer.

Given

$$
W_1,b_1,
$$

we can calculate

$$
H=|XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|,
$$

then

$$
\tilde W_2^*=\tilde H^+Y,
$$

and finally the exact minimum training loss.

Therefore:

### Solved

* Output weights \(W_2\)
* Output bias \(b_2\)
* Minimum loss for any fixed hidden representation
* Scalar one-neuron reparameterization
* Exact scaling symmetry

### Remaining

The difficult part is

$$
\boxed{
\min_{W_1,b_1}
\left\|
(I-P_{\tilde H})Y
\right\|_F^2
}
$$

because

$$
H=|XW_1+\mathbf{1}b_1^T|.
$$

The difficulty is therefore concentrated in the first layer and, specifically, in the activation-induced sign patterns.

---

# 10. Why We Do Not Need Gradient Descent

The absolute-value activation gives us a piecewise-linear structure.

Define the augmented input matrix

$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
X & \mathbf{1}
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(d+1)}.
$$

For a single hidden neuron define

$$
\theta=
\begin{bmatrix}
w\\
b
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its preactivation is

$$
z=A\theta.
$$

The hidden output is

$$
h=|A\theta|.
$$

For a fixed sign pattern

$$
s\in\{-1,+1\}^n,
$$

define

$$
D_s=\operatorname{diag}(s).
$$

Inside that activation region,

$$
h=D_sA\theta.
$$

Define

$$
B_s=D_sA.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
h=B_s\theta.
}
$$

Thus the nonlinear activation disappears once the sign pattern is fixed.

---

# 11. Single-Hidden-Neuron Coordinate Optimization

Suppose all other hidden neurons are fixed.

Let their contribution be

$$
\hat Y_{-j}.
$$

Define the residual

$$
R_j=Y-\hat Y_{-j}.
$$

We now optimize the \(j\)-th hidden neuron:

$$
\min_{\theta,a}
\left\|
R_j-|A\theta|a^T
\right\|_F^2,
$$

where

$$
a\in\mathbb{R}^m
$$

is the output weight vector of that hidden neuron.

For fixed \(\theta\), define

$$
h=|A\theta|.
$$

Then

$$
\min_a
\|R_j-ha^T\|_F^2
$$

is ordinary least squares.

The optimal output weight is

$$
\boxed{
a^*
=
\frac{h^TR_j}{h^Th}
}
$$

when \(h^Th\neq0\).

---

# 12. Eliminating the Output Weight of One Neuron

Substituting \(a^*\) back into the objective gives

$$
F(\theta)
=
\|R_j\|_F^2
-
\frac{
\|h^TR_j\|_2^2
}{
h^Th
}.
$$

Therefore minimizing the residual is equivalent to maximizing

$$
\boxed{
\frac{
\|h^TR_j\|_2^2
}{
h^Th
}.
}
$$

Since

$$
h=|A\theta|,
$$

the remaining optimization is

$$
\boxed{
\max_\theta
\frac{
\theta^T
B_s^TR_jR_j^TB_s
\theta
}{
\theta^T
B_s^TB_s
\theta
}
}
$$

inside a fixed sign region.

---

# 13. Generalized Rayleigh Quotient

Define

$$
C_s
=
B_s^TR_jR_j^TB_s
$$

and

$$
D_s
=
B_s^TB_s.
$$

Then the objective becomes

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\theta^TC_s\theta}
{\theta^TD_s\theta}.
}
$$

This is a generalized Rayleigh quotient.

The unconstrained maximizer is obtained from the generalized eigenvalue problem

$$
\boxed{
C_s\theta
=
\lambda D_s\theta.
}
$$

The maximum generalized eigenvalue gives the best value of the quotient for that linearized region.

---

# 14. Important Simplification

Because

$$
B_s=D_s^{(\text{sign})}A
$$

and

$$
D_s^{(\text{sign})T}D_s^{(\text{sign})}=I,
$$

we obtain

$$
B_s^TB_s
=
A^TA.
$$

Therefore the denominator does not actually depend on the sign pattern.

Hence

$$
\boxed{
D_s=A^TA.
}
$$

Only the numerator depends on the activation region:

$$
C_s
=
B_s^TR_jR_j^TB_s.
$$

Thus the problem inside a sign region becomes

$$
\boxed{
C_s\theta
=
\lambda A^TA\theta.
}
$$

This is a major simplification.

---

# 15. The Crucial Feasibility Condition

The generalized eigenvector is only valid if it actually belongs to the sign region used to derive it.

The assumed sign pattern requires

$$
\operatorname{sign}(A\theta)=s.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
D_sA\theta\ge0
$$

with strict inequalities for an interior region.

Therefore a generalized eigenvector \(\theta_s\) is a valid interior candidate only if

$$
\boxed{
D_sA\theta_s\ge0.
}
$$

If the eigenvector satisfies the sign constraints, then it gives the exact optimum of the unconstrained Rayleigh quotient inside that region.

If it violates them, it cannot be used as the solution for that region.

---

# 16. What Happens at Activation Boundaries?

A difficulty remains.

The optimum within a particular activation region may occur on its boundary.

A boundary occurs when one or more samples satisfy

$$
A_i\theta=0.
$$

At such a point, the sign pattern changes.

Therefore, checking only the interior generalized eigenvector is not enough to guarantee every possible optimum.

A complete exact procedure must consider:

1. Interior activation regions.
2. Their boundaries.
3. Lower-dimensional intersections of boundaries.

This creates a hierarchical structure of activation cells and faces.

The absolute-value network is therefore naturally connected to the geometry of hyperplane arrangements.

---

# 17. Activation Regions as Hyperplane Arrangements

For a single neuron,

$$
A\theta=0
$$

defines \(n\) hyperplanes in the \((d+1)\)-dimensional parameter space.

These hyperplanes partition parameter space into regions.

Inside each region, the sign pattern is fixed.

For \(p=d+1\) parameter dimensions, the number of regions induced by \(n\) hyperplanes is at most

$$
\boxed{
\sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{n}{k}
}
$$

under the general-position upper bound.

Therefore the problem is not arbitrary nonlinear optimization.

It is a finite collection of piecewise-algebraic problems, although the number of regions can become very large.

This gives a potential exact strategy when the input dimension \(d\) is small.

---

# 18. Gradient-Free Multi-Neuron Optimization

For multiple hidden neurons, we can exploit the single-neuron solution through coordinate/block optimization.

For neuron \(j\):

1. Remove its current contribution.
2. Compute the residual

$$
R_j=Y-\hat Y_{-j}.
$$

3. Solve

$$
\min_{\theta_j,a_j}
\|R_j-|A\theta_j|a_j^T\|_F^2
$$

without gradients.

4. Enumerate or search activation regions \(s\).

5. For each candidate region, solve

$$
C_s\theta=\lambda A^TA\theta.
$$

6. Check whether the resulting \(\theta\) satisfies the sign constraints.

7. Evaluate the actual training loss.

8. Select the best feasible candidate.

9. Update neuron \(j\).

Then repeat for

$$
j=1,\ldots,h.
$$

This gives a gradient-free block coordinate optimization procedure.

---

# 19. Why This Is Different from Ordinary Coordinate Descent

Ordinary coordinate descent might optimize a parameter using small numerical steps.

Here, the goal is much stronger.

For each hidden neuron, we attempt to solve its entire subproblem algebraically.

The procedure is approximately:

$$
\boxed{
\text{activation region}
\rightarrow
\text{generalized eigenproblem}
\rightarrow
\text{feasibility check}
\rightarrow
\text{exact candidate}
}
$$

rather than

$$
\text{gradient}
\rightarrow
\text{small step}
\rightarrow
\text{gradient}
\rightarrow\cdots
$$

If the neuron subproblem is solved globally, each coordinate update cannot increase the training loss.

Thus the sequence satisfies

$$
L^{(k+1)}\le L^{(k)}.
$$

It converges to a coordinate-wise stationary solution under appropriate conditions, although this does **not** guarantee the global optimum of the entire neural network.

---

# 20. Multiple Restarts

Because the overall neural network remains nonconvex, one coordinate-wise solution may not be the best one.

Therefore we can use multiple gradient-free initializations:

$$
\theta^{(0)}_1,\ldots,\theta^{(0)}_h
$$

and run the block-coordinate procedure from each initialization.

This produces solutions

$$
\mathcal{S}_1,\mathcal{S}_2,\ldots,\mathcal{S}_K.
$$

For each solution, evaluate:

$$
L_{\text{train}}
$$

and, importantly,

$$
L_{\text{validation}}.
$$

The final model can be selected according to validation performance rather than training loss alone.

---

# 21. Training vs Validation Selection

The optimization itself should use the training set:

$$
\min L_{\text{train}}.
$$

After obtaining several candidate local solutions, evaluate each on a separate validation set:

$$
L_{\text{val}}^{(k)}
=
\frac1{n_{\text{val}}}
\|\hat Y^{(k)}_{\text{val}}-Y_{\text{val}}\|_F^2.
$$

Then select

$$
\boxed{
k^*
=
\arg\min_k L_{\text{val}}^{(k)}.
}
$$

This allows us to use the collection of different local solutions as candidate models.

---

# 22. Is This Faster Than Gradient Descent?

Not necessarily.

There is a fundamental trade-off.

Gradient descent has relatively cheap iterations:

$$
O(\text{forward/backward pass})
$$

but may require many iterations.

The proposed algebraic method can make much larger, exact updates, but activation-region enumeration can be expensive.

For low-dimensional inputs, the number of activation regions may be manageable, making the algebraic approach attractive.

For high-dimensional inputs, the number of regions can become enormous, and gradient-based methods may be much faster.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Low }d:
\text{ exact/region-based methods may be practical}
}
$$

while

$$
\boxed{
\text{High }d:
\text{ gradient methods may be computationally preferable}.
}
$$

The purpose of the algebraic method is therefore not automatically to outperform gradient descent computationally.

Its advantages are:

* no gradients,
* interpretable optimization,
* exact solutions for subproblems,
* explicit handling of activation regions,
* mathematical characterization of candidate solutions,
* potential exact solutions in low-dimensional settings.

---

# 23. Current Status of the Problem

The original optimization problem was

$$
\min_{W_1,b_1,W_2,b_2}
\left\|
|XW_1+\mathbf1b_1^T|W_2
+\mathbf1b_2^T-Y
\right\|_F^2.
$$

We have reduced it substantially.

The output layer has an exact closed-form solution:

$$
\boxed{
\tilde W_2^*=\tilde H^+Y.
}
$$

Therefore the remaining nontrivial optimization is

$$
\boxed{
\min_{W_1,b_1}
\left\|
(I-P_{\tilde H})Y
\right\|_F^2.
}
$$

For a single hidden neuron in a coordinate update, the output weight can also be eliminated exactly, yielding

$$
\boxed{
\max_\theta
\frac{
\theta^TB_s^TRR^TB_s\theta
}{
\theta^TA^TA\theta
}.
}
$$

For a fixed activation region, this becomes the generalized eigenvalue problem

$$
\boxed{
B_s^TRR^TB_s\theta
=
\lambda A^TA\theta.
}
$$

The candidate must satisfy the activation-region constraints

$$
\boxed{
D_sA\theta\ge0.
}
$$

This provides a completely gradient-free algebraic route for optimizing individual hidden neurons.

---

# 24. What Has Not Yet Been Proven

Several important questions remain open.

### 24.1 Global optimum of the full network

We have not yet derived a general closed-form global solution for arbitrary

$$
X,Y,h,d,m.
$$

The multi-neuron interactions make the full problem nonconvex.

### 24.2 All local minima

The region-based approach gives a systematic way to generate candidates, but proving that every local minimum has been found requires complete treatment of:

* all activation regions,
* all activation boundaries,
* lower-dimensional faces,
* interactions between hidden neurons.

### 24.3 Boundary optimization

If the generalized eigenvector for an activation region is infeasible, the constrained optimum may lie on a boundary where

$$
A_i\theta=0.
$$

A complete exact algorithm must recursively handle these boundary cases.

### 24.4 Computational complexity

Although the method is algebraically elegant, enumerating all activation regions can become expensive as \(n\) and \(d\) increase.

Thus practical implementations may need:

* region sampling,
* intelligent region generation,
* pruning,
* symmetry reduction,
* warm starts,
* branch-and-bound,
* or other combinatorial optimization techniques.

---

# 25. Main Insight

The central mathematical insight is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{The absolute-value activation creates the nonconvexity,}
}
$$

but simultaneously

$$
\boxed{
\text{its piecewise-linear structure makes the problem algebraically tractable within each activation region.}
}
$$

Therefore, instead of treating the network as an arbitrary nonlinear optimization problem, we can view it as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{finite/structured activation regions}
+
\text{exact algebraic optimization within each region}.
}
$$

For the output layer, this gives ordinary least squares.

For a single hidden-neuron coordinate update, it gives a generalized Rayleigh quotient and therefore a generalized eigenvalue problem.

This leads to the broader research direction:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Piecewise-linear neural network}
\rightarrow
\text{activation-region decomposition}
\rightarrow
\text{exact local subproblem solutions}
\rightarrow
\text{gradient-free optimization}.
}
$$

# 27. Final Perspective

The important result so far is not a single closed-form expression for the entire neural network.

Instead, we have obtained a hierarchy of increasingly tractable problems:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Full NN}
\rightarrow
\text{exact output-layer elimination}
\rightarrow
\text{single-neuron optimization}
\rightarrow
\text{activation-region decomposition}
\rightarrow
\text{generalized eigenvalue problem}.
}
$$

This provides a promising route toward **partial closed-form solutions for neural networks with absolute-value activations**, while avoiding gradient descent entirely.

The remaining research challenge is to determine how activation-region feasibility and boundary cases can be handled efficiently enough to characterize either:

1. all local minima,
2. all coordinate-wise minima,
3. a provably complete finite candidate set, or
4. an efficient practical algorithm that generates strong local minima.

The key structural property enabling this approach is the piecewise-linear nature of the absolute-value activation.
